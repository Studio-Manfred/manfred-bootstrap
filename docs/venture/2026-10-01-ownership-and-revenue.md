# Ownership and revenue-model recommendations

> **Purpose:** the pre-lawyer conversation. Sketch a fair, defensible structure for who owns what, who earns what, and how the value flows — before anyone drafts a shareholders' agreement. Grounded in the current SeventyOne Group reality (three firms, multiple owners each, Jens as sole IP holder of the new methodology).
>
> **Not legal advice.** Every number and clause here needs a Swedish AB-qualified lawyer to review before anything is signed. This doc is a starting position for that conversation, not a term sheet.

## 1. The picture today

**SeventyOne Group** (informal umbrella, not a legal entity itself):

| Firm | Legal shape | Owners today | Jens's position |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Studio Manfred AB** | Aktiebolag | 3 owners (Jens + 2) | 1/3 equity, 1/3 revenue share |
| **SeventyOne Consulting AB** | Aktiebolag | David Cederblad + Håkan Appelgren (+ possibly others) | none |
| **Mather Studio** | Legal form TBC | Moa Bernhardsson (+ possibly others) | none |

**IP situation for the new venture:**

- **Jens personally is the sole IP holder** of `manfred-bootstrap`, the role system, the DS-first pattern, and the WoW discipline
- The IP was developed on Jens's own time under Studio Manfred's brand but has not been formally assigned to Studio Manfred AB
- Studio Manfred AB as a company has not (yet) contributed anything to the IP beyond the brand association
- The other two Manfred owners have not contributed to the IP

**Meaning:** the founder-inventor equity is legitimately Jens's alone. The value-add of the other parties is going to come through **channel, capital, talent, and change-management practice** — not through IP contribution.

## 3. Three recommended structures

Ranked by my preference. Each is fair and defensible in different ways; all three need Swedish-legal review before commitment.

### Recommendation A — Personal-founder + firm-shareholder hybrid (my preference)

Jens holds personal founder equity for his IP + operating role. Each of the three firms is a corporate shareholder for its firm-level contribution. Option pool for future hires and advisors.

| Party | Equity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Jens Wedin (personal)** | 45% | Founder + sole IP contributor + operating CEO. Vests 4 years, 1-year cliff, standard. |
| **Studio Manfred AB** | 5% | Recognises the brand + methodology-adjacent contribution. Small because most of Manfred's value flows through Jens's personal stake. |
| **SeventyOne Consulting AB** | 15% | Channel + change-management practice + advisory. Contribution weighted by client-introduction volume in year 1. |
| **Mather Studio** | 15% | Talent pool + delivery capacity. Contribution weighted by embed-hours contracted in year 1. |
| **Option pool** | 20% | Future hires (delivery ops, product eng, DevRel). Vests as awarded. |

**Pros:**
- Recognises Jens's IP fairly — 45% for the founder-inventor with sole IP is on the low-normal side of standard early-stage founder equity, defensible against investor pushback
- Preserves the Manfred co-owners' interest via the firm's 5% stake
- Each Group firm has real skin in the game (15% each) — enough to motivate active contribution, not so much that Jens loses operating control
- Option pool is fundraisable-shape from day 1 — no restructuring needed if we take a seed round in 2027

**Cons:**
- The 5% to Studio Manfred AB may feel small to Jens's co-owners in Manfred — worth a conversation before this lands
- Firm-level shareholders mean any change in a firm's ownership (someone leaves, someone joins) propagates into the new venture's cap table — governance clause needed
- Deadlock risk if the four voting blocs disagree — needs a deadlock-breaker clause (usually: founder-CEO tiebreaker in operational matters; unanimous-with-buy-out for strategic)

**Why I recommend this:** it's the shape a professional investor would accept in a Series-A term sheet with the fewest changes required. Founder-CEO majority-ish equity, small strategic-holder positions, clear option pool. Also the simplest to explain.

### Recommendation B — All-personal cap table

Individual owners across all firms get personal equity based on their contribution. No firm-level shareholding.

| Party | Equity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Jens Wedin** | 45% | Same as A |
| **David Cederblad (personal)** | 8% | SeventyOne co-founder; strategic advisor role |
| **Håkan Appelgren (personal)** | 8% | SeventyOne co-founder / CEO; enterprise-channel captain |
| **Moa Bernhardsson (personal)** | 12% | Delivery-side co-founder; heavier operational role |
| **Manfred co-owner #2 (personal)** | 3% | Recognition + goodwill; small |
| **Manfred co-owner #3 (personal)** | 3% | Same |
| **Mather other principal (if any, personal)** | 1% | Placeholder for named Mather owners |
| **Option pool** | 20% | Same as A |

**Pros:**
- Cleanest cap table for investors (no nested corporate ownership)
- Each individual has direct financial interest in the venture's success — motivation is aligned at the individual level, not the firm level
- Simpler to handle if a firm dissolves or a founder leaves that firm
- More flexible in year 1 — individuals can be added/removed without touching other firms

**Cons:**
- More complex to negotiate (each individual gets their own conversation about their stake)
- Doesn't recognise the firm-level assets (SeventyOne's brand, Mather's talent pool) as contributions — only the founders' personal effort
- May feel awkward to the firms if their name is on the marketing but their entity isn't on the cap table
- Voting-block complexity — 7-way agreement is harder than 4-way

**Why not first choice:** the individual-level structure obscures the firm-level contributions we're actually leveraging (SeventyOne's practice, Mather's network). Investors would ask "why isn't the practice on the cap table?" and the answer is complicated.

### Recommendation C — Holding-company + operating-company (two-tier)

A Swedish holding AB owned by the three firms + Jens. The holding company 100% owns the operating AB (the actual venture). Cleaner tax treatment (Swedish participation-exemption rules on dividends between AB's); investor-friendly if we ever take institutional money and want to spin the operating co into a separate structure.

**Holding AB cap table:**

| Party | Equity in Holding | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Jens Wedin (personal) | 45% | |
| Studio Manfred AB | 5% | |
| SeventyOne Consulting AB | 15% | |
| Mather Studio | 15% | |
| Option pool at Operating AB level | 20% | Awarded at Op-co, not Holding |

**Pros:**
- Cleanest for **dividend flow** — profits move from Operating AB to Holding AB tax-free under participation exemption, then get distributed to individual shareholders efficiently
- Cleanest for **exit** — sell Operating AB and Holding AB shareholders share the proceeds without a second layer of tax
- Investor-friendly — a Series A can buy into Operating AB directly, with Holding AB retaining majority; less dilution for the founders
- Cleanest for **shared-IP terms** — if the group wants specific IP-licence structures, those can sit at Operating AB layer without touching shareholder rights

**Cons:**
- More setup cost (two entities to file, two sets of accounting, two board resolutions)
- More ongoing admin (annual reports for both; audits if thresholds exceeded)
- Overkill until we have real revenue — usually adopted at Series A, not at founding
- Adds a governance layer (Holding AB board decides on Operating AB matters) — slower decision-making

**Why not first choice:** it's the right structure for later, not now. Adopt Recommendation A first; migrate to Recommendation C at the Series A / first paying-year threshold if the tax + investor case justifies it.

### Not recommended — "revenue-share only, no equity yet"

I considered a "prove-it-first" model: everyone gets a revenue-share agreement (contractual) for 12 months; equity discussions defer to end of 2027 once we know the wedge worked. Pros: less to argue about now. Cons: creates massive negotiation weight later (people who've been earning revenue-share will want equity on richer terms than they'd accept today); doesn't give the firms real skin-in-the-game motivation; delays every fundraising conversation. Reject.

## 4. Revenue model recommendations

Independent of the equity structure, the money has to flow in a way that respects each contributor's economics. Four flows to design:

### 4.1 Retainer + pilot revenue → Operating AB

All client cash goes to the venture's operating entity. From there:

- Operating costs (delivery ops, tooling, salaries) — first out
- Retained earnings for R&D + working capital
- Dividends distributed per equity split — annual, at board discretion

**Recommendation:** in year 1, retain ~40–60% of profits inside the venture for reinvestment. Distribute the rest per cap table. Standard startup posture.

### 4.2 Referral fees for client introductions

When SeventyOne Consulting or Mather Studio introduces a client that converts:

- **First engagement:** 10% referral fee to the introducing firm, off the pilot revenue only
- **Retainer conversions:** 5% for the first 6 months of retainer revenue, then step to zero
- **Cap:** €25K per client, lifetime

This is on top of equity, not instead. It's a real cash motivator to actively introduce clients rather than passively hold shares.

**Why capped:** without the cap, a lucky referral in year 1 becomes disproportionate income for years afterward, and the venture bleeds margin on its best customers.

### 4.3 Mather-contractor billing pass-through

When Mather-network contractors deliver embedded work through the venture:

- Contractor bills the venture (not the client)
- Venture bills the client at a markup — recommended **50% markup** on contractor day-rate (industry-standard for pass-through delivery)
- Of that 50% markup: **20% to Mather** (Mather's contractor-management fee), **80% to Operating AB** (venture's delivery margin)

**Numbers:** contractor at €800/day → venture bills client €1,200/day. €400 markup: €80 to Mather, €320 to Operating AB.

**Why this works:** Mather's economics are protected (their contractors get their full rate + Mather gets its usual management cut); the venture earns a delivery margin without hiring risk; the client sees a coherent single invoice.

### 4.4 IP-licence royalty from the venture to Studio Manfred AB

Symbolic + real. The venture's core IP (bootstrap, roles, DS-first) is assigned by Jens personally to Operating AB. **However**, an IP licence back to Studio Manfred AB lets Manfred continue using the methodology on its own client work — with a small royalty flowing to the venture (say 2% of Manfred's revenue that touches the methodology).

**Why:** protects Manfred's ongoing consulting practice + creates a small annuity for the venture. Also positions the venture as the canonical owner of the methodology, which matters for investor optics and any future exit.

Same clause available to SeventyOne + Mather if they want to use the methodology on their own client work — 2% royalty either way.

## 5. Founder equity mechanics (Jens-specific)

Because Jens is the sole IP holder + operating founder, the founder-equity clauses need real care:

- **Vesting.** 4-year cliff-vest with 1-year cliff, standard. If Jens leaves in year 1, his stake unvests; the venture is protected.
- **IP assignment.** At entity formation, Jens personally assigns the current IP to Operating AB in exchange for the founder equity grant. Documented as a written IP-assignment agreement, reviewed by counsel.
- **Non-compete.** Jens can't set up a competing venture for 24 months post-departure; standard.
- **Accelerated vesting on change of control.** If the venture is acquired, Jens's unvested shares accelerate — standard startup protection.
- **Related-party disclosure.** Jens is a 1/3 owner of Studio Manfred AB, which is a 5% shareholder of the venture. This is a related-party relationship that needs disclosure and abstention from voting on any transaction between the venture and Studio Manfred AB (e.g., the royalty clause). Standard corporate-governance requirement.

## 6. Governance recommendations

- **Board:** 4 seats initially — Jens (founder-CEO), 1 SeventyOne rep, 1 Mather rep, 1 independent (mutually agreed, added within 12 months)
- **Operational decisions:** Jens as CEO decides
- **Strategic decisions** (fundraising, hiring above VP level, acquisition offers, IP-licence changes): board majority
- **Fundamental decisions** (dissolution, sale of >25% of shares, change of venture purpose): supermajority (75%)
- **Deadlock breaker:** if board is deadlocked on operational matters for >30 days, CEO decision stands; on strategic matters, external mediator (mutually agreed at incorporation) is called
- **Exit alignment:** drag-along rights (if 75% of shareholders vote to sell, minority must go along); tag-along rights (if any shareholder sells, others can join proportionally)
- **Right of first refusal:** if any firm shareholder wants to sell its stake, other shareholders + venture have ROFR at the offered price

## 7. What Jens keeps + what Jens gives up

Because this is Jens's IP, his position matters most.

**Keeps:**
- 45% personal equity (or thereabouts)
- Operating CEO role
- Right to continue running Studio Manfred AB alongside (with the 2% royalty for methodology use)
- The reputation and brand of the founder-inventor
- Full editorial control over the methodology's evolution

**Gives up:**
- Sole IP ownership → assigned to Operating AB (in exchange for 45% of the venture)
- Freedom to compete (24-month non-compete)
- Some operational speed (board oversight on strategic decisions)
- The right to unilaterally decide to shut it all down (needs board approval)

**Net:** Jens trades sole ownership of a pre-revenue methodology for majority-adjacent ownership of a venture with real distribution, real delivery capacity, real change-management wrap, and a shared incentive to make it succeed. If the venture is even mildly successful, this trade is heavily in Jens's favour. If it fails, Jens has lost some time and a set of options he could have run solo.

## 8. What the Manfred co-owners get

This is the delicate one. The two other Studio Manfred AB owners contributed nothing directly to the new venture, but they own 2/3 of a firm that's now a 5% shareholder. Their upside math:

- If the venture reaches €1M in profits distributed as dividends, Studio Manfred AB receives 5% = €50K
- Of that, the two other Manfred owners share 2/3 = ~€33K collectively
- Jens receives 1/3 of Manfred's share (€16.5K) + his personal 45% share (€450K)

**This is fair because:** the venture succeeds mostly on Jens's IP + operating work, which was his personal contribution; the Manfred co-owners share a small proportional benefit because their firm's brand + Jens-being-their-partner enabled the founding conversation.

**However** — worth a real conversation with the Manfred co-owners in October to make sure they see it as fair. If they don't, alternatives:
- Increase Studio Manfred AB's stake to 8–10% (dilutes Jens's founder equity to ~40–42%; still defensible)
- Offer them personal advisor equity (2–3% each) in addition to the firm's stake
- Recognise their ongoing consulting work at Manfred more explicitly through the methodology-royalty clause

## 9. What if the group says "no thanks"

If David + Håkan + Moa/Mather decline the fourth-firm structure and prefer a referral relationship only:

- **Jens forms a standalone venture** (Swedish AB), 100% personal equity minus a 15–20% option pool
- **Referral agreements** signed with each Group firm (10% of first-engagement revenue, capped)
- **No IP flowing between firms**; Jens's Manfred consulting continues separately
- **Studio Manfred AB's 5% stake** doesn't exist in this scenario — Jens holds his Manfred share and his venture share as separate things

Clean, simple, fastest to form. Loses the co-ownership synergy but preserves optionality.

## 11. What this doc doesn't cover

Deliberately deferred:

- Specific salary levels for operating roles (needs financial model — D1 in the open questions)
- Exit terms (drag-along thresholds, ROFR mechanics — needs lawyer)
- Class of shares (voting vs. non-voting, preferred vs. common — matters for future fundraising)
- Tax residency structuring (Sweden vs. Luxembourg vs. Delaware — irrelevant until we're at €5M+ revenue)
- Employee stock ownership plan (ESOP) mechanics beyond the 20% pool sizing
- Anti-dilution provisions for future rounds (broad-based weighted-average recommended when the time comes)

All of these are Q1 2027 / post-wedge-decision problems. Now: pick a structure family (A, B, or C), get a lawyer to review it, and take it to the group.
